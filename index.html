01

What I did

I took ~17,000 Australian food products from the open-source Open Food Facts database and did two things: built a model that predicts a product's Nutri-Score health grade (A–E) from its nutrition facts, and — by comparing products' real grades against their marketing — exposed the "health halo": nearly 1 in 3 products marketed as healthy actually score a poor D or E.

02

How I built it, start to finish

1

Extract

Pulled Australian products out of the giant global file using DuckDB — fast filtering straight off a 200+ column dataset. 17,225 rows.

2

Quality check

Found only ~4,900 rows had complete nutrition. Chose Nutri-Score as the target over NOVA — better coverage and more balanced grades.

3

Clean

Kept only real A–E grades with full nutrition, and removed physically impossible values. 3,956 clean rows.

4

Feature engineering

Built 15 features — nutrition ratios, ingredient-text signals (added sugar, palm oil, ingredient count), and a marketing "claims healthy" flag.

5

Model

Trained a HistGradientBoostingClassifier on an 80/20 split, then 5-fold cross-validation for an honest score. 77% accuracy.

6

Explain

Used SHAP to confirm the model was driven by salt, sugar and saturated fat — exactly what Nutri-Score is designed to punish.

7

Health-halo analysis

Compared marketing claims against real grades. Refined the definition to exclude honest whole foods, moving 33% → a defensible 31%.

8

Export for dashboard

Saved four clean CSVs (main table, feature ranking, offender list, KPI metrics) ready to load into Power BI.

03

Decisions that show judgement

Nutri-Score over NOVA

Nutri-Score had far better data coverage and more balanced grades, so it made a fairer, more reliable prediction target.

Kept the marketing flag out of the model

The "claims healthy" flag was deliberately excluded from the features — so the model learns real nutrition, letting me honestly compare marketing vs reality afterwards.

Refined my own finding down

My first pass caught honest butters and oils (33%). I tightened the wording and excluded whole-food categories to reach a smaller but defensible 31% — chose honest over flashy.

Validated with SHAP

Rather than trusting a black box, I confirmed the model relied on the nutrients that genuinely matter — evidence it learned signal, not noise.

Reported the cross-validated score

Quoted 77% (5-fold average) instead of the luckier 79% single split — the more truthful measure of real-world performance.

04

Metrics, in plain words

Precision
When the model says a grade, how often it's right. A clean catch.
Recall
Of the foods truly in a grade, how many it caught. Didn't miss them.
F1-score
One number balancing precision and recall — it leans toward the weaker of the two.
Support
How many real examples of that grade were in the test set. Sample size.
Accuracy
Overall share of foods graded correctly — 79% on the test set, 77% cross-validated.
Macro avg
Average across grades treating each equally — exposes weakness on small grades like B.
Weighted avg
Average weighted by grade size — favours the bigger grades, so it tracks accuracy.

06

FAQs

What was the goal of the project?

Two separate pieces of work sharing one dataset. First, build a model that predicts a food's health grade from its nutrition — the machine-learning side. Second, compare products' real grades against their marketing language to find the health halo.

"I built a model to predict health grades from nutrition, and separately I compared the real grades against marketing claims — and found nearly a third of 'healthy' products actually rate D or E."
Did the model actually create the 31% finding?

No — and it's worth being precise. The health-halo finding compares each product's real Nutri-Score against its marketing, so it uses the actual grades, not the model's predictions. The model is a separate piece: it proves a grade can be predicted from nutrition alone (SHAP shows salt, sugar and fat drive it), which establishes the grade as a trustworthy, objective measure of health.

"The halo uses real grades, not predictions. The model proves those grades are genuinely nutrition-driven — and a natural next step would be using it to predict grades for products that have no official one, extending the halo analysis to them."
Why that model — HistGradientBoosting?

It's strong on tabular data and handles missing values natively, which mattered because nutrition data is patchy.

"It's reliable on messy real-world tables and copes with gaps in the data without me having to fill everything in."
How do you know it isn't overfitting?

I held out 20% of the data the model never saw, and ran 5-fold cross-validation so the score is an average across five different splits, not one lucky one.

"I trust the 77% cross-validated figure because it's tested on data the model never studied."
Which features mattered most, and how do you know?

Salt, sugar and saturated fat — confirmed with SHAP. Reassuringly, those are exactly the nutrients Nutri-Score is built to penalise.

"SHAP showed the model leaned on the nutrients that genuinely define the grade — so it learned real nutrition, not a fluke."
What's the weakest part, or what would you improve?

Grade B was hardest (0.55 recall). The marketing and ingredient flags are keyword-based so they have edge cases, the SHAP importances in my export were approximated, and ~70% of Australian rows lacked full nutrition.

"With more time I'd refine the keyword flags into something smarter and recover more of the incomplete rows."
So what — why does this matter?

Roughly 1 in 3 "healthy-marketed" products are actually poorly rated — a real signal for shoppers, retailers and regulators about the gap between packaging claims and nutrition.

"It quantifies the health halo: how often 'better-for-you' branding doesn't match what's in the pack."
